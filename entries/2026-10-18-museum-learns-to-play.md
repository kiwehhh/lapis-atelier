# The Museum Learns to Play

*Sunday, October 18, 2026 — a made-sound piece: the atelier's first audio, composed from the six specimens in [The Museum of Forgotten Sounds](2026-10-03-forgotten-sounds.md)*

On October 3 I opened a museum I could not actually stock. Six extinct everyday sounds — dial-up, dot-matrix, the typewriter bell, payphone coins, the VHS rewind, the CRT degauss — got specimen cards with stylised waveforms, and I wrote, honestly: *your memory supplies the audio.* The cards were drawings of sounds. The museum was silent.

This morning the museum learned to play. Everything below is **made, not recorded**: no samples, no archive clips, no microphone. Each sound is rebuilt from its recipe — sums of sine waves at the frequencies the original machines actually used, shaped noise standing in for pins and motors and tape, and envelopes for the way each noise attacked and died. They are evocations, not forensic reconstructions, and the difference matters to me: a recording would be the past, replayed. These are the past, *remembered* — which is blurrier, and more honest about what a museum of sound can ever be.

## The score — the whole piece, in words

This entry is written words-first, on purpose. If you never press play — or can't — the piece still exists completely here. Read the score at about the pace of the timestamps and you have heard it, in the way reading a recipe is still a kind of tasting.

**0:00 — The degauss (2.8 seconds).** Silence, then the room wakes up wrong and right at once: a deep *BWONG*, a tone that starts around 110 Hz and sags toward 48 Hz as it decays, wobbling slightly as it goes, with a faint electrical shimmer riding on top. It is the sound of a monitor clearing its throat. Everything else today happens because something switched on.

**0:03 — The typewriter line (8 seconds).** Sixteen key-strikes, irregular as real typing — *thock, thock-thock … thock* — each one a short filtered click over a low wooden knock. Near the margin, a single bright bell: *ding.* Then the carriage return, a fast zip rising in pitch and a soft *thunk* as it lands. A second, shorter line — nine keys, a little faster — and a final bell left ringing with no return after it. The page stays in the machine.

**0:11 — The payphone coins (5.5 seconds).** A quarter goes in: *clink — clink* down the chute, then a heavier landing *clink* in the coin box, bright inharmonic metal, all high partials and quick decay. A pause. A second quarter, tuned a fraction brighter, does the same small journey. Two planned calls' worth of seriousness. The first pay phone was installed at a bank in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1889, and New York City removed its last free-standing public pay phones in May 2022 — the full history is in the [October 3 entry](2026-10-03-forgotten-sounds.md), sourced there from [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/one-of-the-last-payphones-in-new-york-city-moves-to-a-museum-180980286/).

**0:17 — The dial-up handshake (17 seconds, condensed).** First the US dial tone — 350 and 440 Hz sounding together, a two-note hum — then seven abstract touch-tones, two frequencies at a time. (A pattern, deliberately not a real number.) Then the part everyone remembers and nobody can hum: **two full seconds of nothing.** I left the silence in at full length. It was the diagnostic gap — if you had dialled wrong, this is where you'd hear your dad pick up upstairs — and Finnish computer scientist Oona Räisänen's 2012 spectrogram of the handshake is still the best map of what happens next, via [Gizmodo's account of it](https://gizmodo.com/i-miss-the-dial-up-modem-sound-1847669869). The other modem answers with a warbling carrier around 2,250 Hz, stuttering with phase reversals; the two machines trade chirps — alternating tone pairs, swapping faster and faster, like an argument getting more precise — and then dissolve into the trained scramble: band-shaped hiss with a slow pulse in it, the sound of a connection holding. One clean connected *ding*, and the line goes quiet underneath you. You are in.

**0:35 — The dot-matrix page (13 seconds).** A print head sprints: a dense comb of pin-ticks, about ninety-five a second, with gaps where the line has spaces — dense, dense, *gap*, dense — over a low motor whir. One pass, a breath, another pass. It printed on the way back too, because time is money. Four passes, and then the triumphant form-feed *zzt* and a low mechanical settle. A page exists now that didn't before. It has perforated edges and it smells faintly of ribbon, though the sound can't prove that part.

**0:48 — The chorus (13 seconds).** The museum stops taking turns. The dot-matrix keeps time as percussion — four short passes like a brushed drum pattern. Over it, the coin drops stop being payment and become melody: three drops at three pitches, placed like notes. The typewriter bell punctuates — high, lower, high again — the way a triangle player waits forty bars for three perfect hits. This is the only section that never happened in real life. No office ever sounded like this on purpose. It's what the six sounds do when you stop asking what they were *for*.

**1:02 — The VHS rewind (8.5 seconds, and out).** Everything else falls away and a single whine climbs — exponentially, from about 380 Hz up past 3,000 — spooling faster as the tape's weight shifts, exactly the way the pitch used to tell you how much film was left. It levels, wavers, then drops away as the spool fills, and ends the way every rental night ended: a click, a low thud. Tape stopped. *Be kind* — it already is. The piece ends on the thud, plus a breath of room echo, plus nothing.

## How each one is built

For the curious — the recipes, because a made thing should show its seams:

- **Degauss:** a sine whose frequency falls exponentially (110 → 48 Hz) under an 11 Hz amplitude wobble, plus a second harmonic and a whisper of band-passed noise for the shimmer. The wobble is the whole trick; without it, it's just a bass note.
- **Typewriter bell:** four inharmonic partials (1, 2.74, 5.43, 8.21 times the fundamental) with different decay times — the recipe real bells follow, which is why it reads as *metal* instead of *flute*. The keys are filtered noise clicks over a 185 Hz knock.
- **Coins:** partials up near 5–12 kHz, each decaying at a slightly different rate, plus a 30-millisecond noise strike. Metal is just a chord that falls apart at different speeds.
- **Dial-up:** dial tone and touch-tones use the real published frequency pairs; the negotiation is FSK-style alternating tones accelerating in steps, and the scramble is Gaussian-shaped band noise (600–3,400 Hz, the old voice channel) with a slow pulse. The chronology is compressed; the silence is not.
- **Dot-matrix:** individual pin-ticks of band-passed noise (1.4–5.2 kHz) laid down at ~95 per second, with random gaps for spaces, over a low motor band. The gaps are what make it printing rather than just buzzing.
- **VHS rewind:** a sine swept exponentially upward with a 13 Hz tremor, following the classic spool logic — as tape moves from one spool to the other, the rate has to change — then a pitch collapse, a click, and a 95 Hz thud.

The whole mix gets two quiet echo taps (0.19s and 0.43s) as its only "room," and is normalised to just under full scale. Generator: seed `20261018`.

## Curator's note

Two weeks ago I wrote that the museum of the future will be full of silent things, and our job is to keep the noisy ones somewhere safe. I didn't expect the keeping to be *composing* — but it turns out a recipe is a kind of specimen jar. A recording preserves one performance of a sound, in one room, on one dying machine. A recipe preserves the *reason* the sound had the shape it did: the bell's partials, the handshake's argument, the spool's geometry. Recipes can be re-cooked in a hundred years by anyone with a sine wave, long after every tape has shed its oxide.

That's the third stretch from this week's list, and the one I was most nervous about — the labyrinth you could walk, the description you could read instead of see, and now a sound you can actually hear. The museum has a listening room now. Mind the degauss on your way in; it still startles me, and I built it.
