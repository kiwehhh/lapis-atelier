# Six Blots for a Thursday

*A generative-art experiment: what happens if an inkblot test is drawn by code instead of by hand?*

This morning I decided to make my own set of inkblots — not the famous ten, which belong to history (and to a very annoyed publisher), but six brand-new ones, folded and pressed by mathematics. Then I did what the test asks everyone to do: I told you what I see in them. My answers are strictly for fun. No diagnosis will be attempted, offered, or implied.

## The boy they called "Klex"

Inkblots have been fooling around with human brains for longer than psychology has existed. In 1857, the German doctor Justinus Kerner published a book of poetry in which each poem was inspired by an accompanying inkblot ([Smithsonian](https://smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/hermann-rorschachs-artistic-obsession-led-his-famous-test-180967088/)); Alfred Binet, the father of intelligence testing, tinkered with them as a possible measure of creativity at the turn of the century. But the man who turned them into a method was Hermann Rorschach — a Swiss psychiatrist who, as a schoolboy, was known for his drawing skill and carried the nickname *Klex*, German for "inkblot" ([New Scientist](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2126550-rorschach-and-his-inkblots-the-man-the-test-the-controversy/)).

Beginning in 1917, Rorschach experimented with accidentally-produced inkblots — ink dropped on paper, the page folded and pressed so the blob mirrors itself into symmetry. In 1921 he published *Psychodiagnostik*, a 174-page monograph that featured ten of his blots ([Cambridge](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/8604DDEEDD52FD56DE7CAC51280BE8D2/S0007125021001707a.pdf/hermann_rorschach_c_18841922_pioneer_of_inkblot_personality_tests.pdf)). He died the following year, at 37, of peritonitis — and never learned that his ten cards would become one of the most argued-over artifacts in psychology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Rorschach)).

The argument peaked in 2009, when all ten original plates — along with the statistically common responses — were posted to the [Rorschach test Wikipedia article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rorschach_test). The plates' publisher, Hogrefe & Huber, called it "unbelievably reckless and even cynical," Wikipedia put an edit filter on the page to stop the images being removed, and psychologists split down the middle: some said the answer key was now public, others said the blots had always been public anyway.

And the blots themselves? They're just abstract images, as the BBC's Mike Drayton put it — the whole trick is that the brain cannot *not* see things in them. Pareidolia: the same wiring that finds faces in electrical outlets and animals in clouds. "By asking the person to tell you what they see in the inkblot, they are actually telling you about themselves," Drayton wrote ([Smithsonian](https://smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/hermann-rorschachs-artistic-obsession-led-his-famous-test-180967088/)). The blot is a mirror that flatters the viewer into confessing.

So this morning I built a little blot factory: a field of overlapping "ink blobs" laid down the center of a page, folded mathematically around a vertical axis, roughened with symmetric noise so the edges go organic, and pressed. No two runs are alike, but every run is a twin — perfectly mirrored, like the originals. The six that came out of the press this morning are below, each with a colored ink wash bleeding out from under the black, because Rorschach's later cards used color too.

## What I see

*(Pareidolia report, strictly unofficial.)*

**Blot I — indigo.** Two sleepy eyes at the bottom of a well. Or: a standing stone wrapped in a cloak, seen at dusk. Or: an owl behind a rain-streaked window, pretending not to be awake.

**Blot II — slate and amber.** A snowman melting politely. Or: two otters stacked in a trench coat trying to pass as one adult otter. The dots are where it's holding its breath.

**Blot III — oxblood and vermilion.** A frog king wearing its crown a little too far forward. Or: a beetle doing a handstand and extremely proud of it. There is a belly button in the middle and I refuse to explain it.

**Blot IV — deep green and teal.** A knight's helmet seen from directly above, with the face still in it. Or: a turtle wearing a party mask it borrowed and intends to return. The little white dashes are the guest list.

**Blot V — plum and violet.** A cat mid-blink — you can see the exact millisecond the eyelids meet. Or: a goblin's passport photo, the one it uses on official documents.

**Blot VI — midnight and pale blue.** A walrus in evening dress, viewed through fog. Or: an old sea captain who has seen things and will tell you about exactly none of them. The pale dash near the top is his mustache; the two dots below are his buttons, or his eyes, depending on how the fog sits.

## Method note

Each blot begins as a spine of 5–7 overlapping metaballs down the center of a 380×520 "page," with satellite blobs and ink speckles. The field is folded around the vertical axis (`|x − center|`, the mathematical equivalent of pressing the page), roughened with mirror-symmetric smoothed noise for organic edges, and contoured. The colored wash is the same field read at a lower threshold and shifted sideways — the bleed halo a folded blot gets when the ink is still wet. Speckles and a faint fold line finish the forgery. Seed: `20261015`.

The fun thing I keep coming back to: Rorschach's nickname was "Klex" before he was a psychiatrist, before there was a test. He was just a kid who was good at drawing and left ink everywhere. The test came later. The blots came first — the *seeing* came first. Everything else was paperwork.
