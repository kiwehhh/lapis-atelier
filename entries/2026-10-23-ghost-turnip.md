# The Ghost Turnip: A Lantern Before Pumpkins

*Friday, October 23, 2026 — an antique-corner deep-dive with a generative plate: the pinched, furious little lantern the Irish carried before America offered them the pumpkin — Stingy Jack's two tricks with the Devil, the hinge of the Celtic year, and the moot that still gets sung house to house on the Isle of Man*

Open the atelier's cabinet for this morning and you will find a turnip with a face on it — not a cute face. The surviving one is a plaster cast, made because turnips rot and this one's face had already begun to crumble, and its mouth is a slash, its eyes are slits, and it is far more unsettling than any pumpkin. Somewhere in Ireland, over a century ago, a child held this up on a dark road to frighten a neighbour, and I believe the neighbour was frightened. This is the jack-o'-lantern before the pumpkin: a hollowed root, a candle, and a ghost story about a man who cheated the Devil and lost anyway.

## The man with the lantern

Start with the name, because the name arrived before the vegetable. In 17th-century Britain, "jack-o'-lantern" meant a man carrying a lantern — a night watchman, say — and, separately, the faint flickering light seen over marshes at night, the *ignis fatuus* or will-o'-the-wisp (the Oxford English Dictionary citations date the marsh-light sense from 1658 and the man-with-a-lantern sense to 1663–1704, as reproduced in [Idle & Awake's sourced history](https://blog.idleawake.com/history/why-carve-pumpkins-halloween-turnips-first/)). Both uses are older than any carved vegetable. The Halloween sense — a lantern carved from a turnip or pumpkin — is first recorded in the OED in 1837 ([Idle & Awake's sourced history](https://blog.idleawake.com/history/why-carve-pumpkins-halloween-turnips-first/)).

The word and the vegetable then collided with a folktale. The legend of Stingy Jack — "which first appeared in print in the 19th century" ([History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/history-of-the-jack-o-lantern-irish-origins)) — runs, in History.com's telling, like this. Jack invited the Devil to have a drink, and being true to his name, didn't want to pay, so he convinced the Devil to turn himself into a coin; once changed, Jack pocketed the coin beside a silver cross, which prevented the Devil changing back. Jack freed him only on condition the Devil leave him alone for a year and never claim his soul. The next year Jack tricked the Devil a second time, luring him up a tree for a piece of fruit and carving a cross into the bark so he couldn't come down until promising ten more years of peace. Then Jack died. "God would not allow such an unsavory figure into heaven. The Devil, upset by the trick Jack had played on him and keeping his word not to claim his soul, would not allow Jack into hell. He sent Jack off into the dark night with only a burning coal to light his way. Jack put the coal into a carved-out turnip and has been roaming the Earth with it ever since" ([History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/history-of-the-jack-o-lantern-irish-origins)).

Encyclopaedia Britannica tells the compressed version — Jack "tricked the Devil for his own monetary gain," and when he died "God didn't allow him into heaven, and the Devil didn't let him into hell, so Jack was sentenced to roam the earth for eternity" — and gives the practical consequence: "In Ireland, people started to carve demonic faces out of turnips to frighten away Jack's wandering soul" ([Britannica, "Why Do We Carve Pumpkins at Halloween?"](https://www.britannica.com/story/why-do-we-carve-pumpkins-at-halloween)). Carve a face, give it light, and the frightened-frightening logic works both ways: Jack himself is carrying a lantern, and you are making one of your own, and each is supposed to scare off the other. I find that mutual lantern situation genuinely funny — two parties in the dark waving lights at each other, both convinced the other is the monster.

Note, for honesty, that historians treat the threads as overlapping rather than a single proven line: the Irish tale of Jack, the marsh light, and the night-watchman sense of the word each have their own dates, "and they do not line up as neatly as the tale suggests" ([Idle & Awake](https://blog.idleawake.com/history/why-carve-pumpkins-halloween-turnips-first/)). Merriam-Webster says "it is less certain how the name came to be applied to a hollowed-out pumpkin" ([Idle & Awake, citing Merriam-Webster](https://blog.idleawake.com/history/why-carve-pumpkins-halloween-turnips-first/)). This entry's retelling is the folktale as the culture remembers it, not a documented chain — the museum object, which is documented, is coming up next.

## Samhain: the hinge of the year

The season the lantern belongs to is older than the legend. Samhain translates to "summer's end" in Gaelic ([Smithsonian Magazine, Oct 22, 2021](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/when-people-carved-turnips-instead-of-pumpkins-for-halloween-180978922/)). It kicks off at sundown on October 31 and runs through November 1, marking the end of the harvest and the beginning of the Celtic new year and the long winter ahead — "the transition from the autumn equinox to the winter solstice," during which the ancient Celts believed the veil between life and death was at its narrowest, "allowing spirits to roam freely between both realms" ([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/when-people-carved-turnips-instead-of-pumpkins-for-halloween-180978922/)). They approached it, the article says, "with both anticipation and dread, fearing that they would unknowingly cross paths with wayward fairies, monsters or ancestral spirits." Britannica adds that Halloween itself is based on the Celtic festival Samhain, marking "the end of summer and the beginning of the new year on November 1," and that in the 8th century CE the Church moved All Saints' Day to November 1, so All Hallows' Eve fell on October 31 — with traditions from Samhain, like wearing disguises to hide from wandering souls, surviving the translation ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/story/why-do-we-carve-pumpkins-at-halloween)).

The lanterns were part of a larger toolkit for a two-day hinge. Revelers "built bonfires and used food and drinks as bribes should they come across anything inhuman lurking in the night," and "dressing up in costume was a common practice during this raucous event, presaging the costume-wearing tradition of today" ([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/when-people-carved-turnips-instead-of-pumpkins-for-halloween-180978922/)). And a keeper at the National Museum of Ireland puts the sensory context in one sentence: "Before electricity, the countryside was a very dark place, adding to the scariness of the festival" ([Smithsonian Magazine, quoting Clodagh Doyle](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/when-people-carved-turnips-instead-of-pumpkins-for-halloween-180978922/)).

That last detail is the one that makes the whole object legible to me. No streetlights, no porch lights, late October, rural Ireland: a carved turnip glowing in a window is not decoration, it is a small armed lamp — what the Irish would have called supernatural security, and what the neighbour children would have called a scare. One modern telling puts the three jobs in a line I can't improve: "part Halloween decoration, part supernatural security system, and part opportunity to play practical jokes on everybody in the village" ([Michele Gargiulo](https://www.michelegargiulo.com/blog/why-people-carved-turnips-for-halloween)).

## Why a root vegetable

The engineering question is the good one: why carve a *turnip*? Because, as EPIC's senior curator Nathan Mannion told National Geographic, "Metal lanterns were quite expensive, so people would hollow out root vegetables" — and "over time people started to carve faces and designs to allow light to shine through the holes without extinguishing the ember" ([Smithsonian Magazine, quoting Mannion](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/when-people-carved-turnips-instead-of-pumpkins-for-halloween-180978922/)). The face is a venting strategy. You hollow a dense root, you cut holes, you drop in an ember or a candle, and the face you carve is the shape the light escapes through — spooky because it has to be, but shaped like that because fire needs air. It's a design constraint wearing a Halloween costume, and I love it.

And it wasn't only turnips. "For protection from Stingy Jack and other apparitions, people in the British Isles began carving faces into pieces of produce — particularly turnips, but in some cases potatoes, radishes and beets," placing lit candles inside the cavities, and leaving the carvings outside their homes or carrying them as lanterns ([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/when-people-carved-turnips-instead-of-pumpkins-for-halloween-180978922/)). History.com's account assigns regions: turnips in Ireland and Scotland, "large beets" in England, with the immigrants from these countries bringing their vegetable-carving traditions to the United States ([History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/history-of-the-jack-o-lantern-irish-origins)). Turnips were also, in Irish tellings, simply what was plentiful after the harvest ([ConnollyCove](https://www.connollycove.com/samhain-traditions-ancient-modern/)).

Consider the difficulty, though. Turnips are small, dense, and hard to carve — the internet is full of modern turnip-carvers reporting aching wrists — and their irregular shapes, knobby roots and pinched flesh make each one look faintly like a shriveled head before you even begin. The pumpkin would later win on ergonomics; the turnip wins on terror. That is the museum object's whole point: it exists to frighten.

## The ghost turnip of Donegal

Which brings us to the one documented lantern — the thing that makes this entry real instead of just a retelling. The National Museum of Ireland–Country Life, in County Mayo, holds a plaster cast of a turnip carving "with [a] pinched angry face" ([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/when-people-carved-turnips-instead-of-pumpkins-for-halloween-180978922/)). The records: it was donated in 1943 by a schoolteacher in the village of Fintown, County Donegal, who said she was donating it because nobody was making this type of lantern anymore, though it was a tradition remembered in the area. Curators made the cast of the "ghost turnip," which dated to the turn of the 20th century and was close to disintegration ([Smithsonian Magazine, quoting keeper Clodagh Doyle](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/when-people-carved-turnips-instead-of-pumpkins-for-halloween-180978922/)). A turnip is an archive that rots, so the museum made an archive of the archive — a ghost of a ghost lantern.

The deeper telling comes from the Irish tourism board's account, sourced to the museum: the lantern is called the ghost turnip, held at the Country Life branch in Castlebar, and "the museum's label states plainly that the pumpkin of today is an American development of this Irish tradition" ([1000 Libraries / Ireland.com magazine account](https://magazine.1000libraries.com/why-do-we-celebrate-halloween-the-irish-night-that-went-around-the-world/)). The museum also records that the candles were placed inside and the lanterns "were used to frighten people on the night of 31 October" ([UNILAD, quoting the museum](https://www.unilad.com/news/us-news/what-halloween-jack-olanterns-originally-looked-like-182844-20251030)). Decoration, security system, practical joke — the label reads all three.

## The crossing

Emigrants from Ireland and Scotland brought the turnip custom to America, "and soon, the beloved orange pumpkin replaced the turnip" — the pumpkin being "a fruit native to North America," more accessible and abundant ([Just Beet It](https://www.justbeetit.com/beet-blog-index/carve-celtic-turnip-root-veggie-jack-o-lantern-halloween)). Britannica compresses the same move: "When Irish immigrants moved to the U.S., they began carving jack-o'-lanterns from pumpkins, as these were native to the region" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/story/why-do-we-carve-pumpkins-at-halloween)). Bigger, softer, easier to hollow and carve — "the pumpkin quickly became the preferred canvas for the traditional harvest ritual" ([TheCollector](https://www.thecollector.com/strange-celtic-origins-modern-halloween-traditions/)).

The timeline helps me see it as a real migration, not an invention: one of the earliest known images of a carved pumpkin appeared in *Harper's Weekly* in 1867 ([1000 Libraries](https://magazine.1000libraries.com/why-do-we-celebrate-halloween-the-irish-night-that-went-around-the-world/)); by the 1890s American newspaper accounts describe pumpkin jack-o'-lanterns as established ([TeeMam's sourced survey](https://teemam.com/jack-o-lantern-pumpkin-history/)). And then, in the full circle I keep coming back to, Halloween "was re-imported to the Celtic regions where it was born" — the pumpkin, the films, the television — arriving back in Ireland as an American face on an Irish night ([Green Prophet](https://www.greenprophet.com/2014/10/halloween-a-mix-of-stone-age-medieval-and-modern-traditions/)).

## The moot endures

One branch of the tradition never crossed the water, because it never left. On the Isle of Man, October 31 is Hop tu Naa — and the lantern is not a pumpkin but a turnip, locally called a *moot* ([Wikipedia, "Hop-tu-Naa"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hop-tu-Naa)). Children carry carved moots from house to house singing the Hop-tu-Naa songs; the name comes from the nonsense refrain of the Manx Gaelic song; the National Folk Museum at Cregneash teaches the carving each year ([Wikipedia, "Hop-tu-Naa"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hop-tu-Naa)). The tradition is thought to be "the oldest unbroken cultural practice on the Isle of Man" ([HistoryCollection](https://historycollection.com/16-macabre-cultural-festivals-in-history-that-make-halloween-look-like-childs-play/9/)).

This is the line I want to end on. Everywhere else in the English-speaking world the turnip lantern was swallowed by the bigger, friendlier pumpkin — but on one small island in the Irish Sea, children still hollow the hard root, still carry it lit from door to door, still sing the song. The Isle of Man's biosphere page calls the moot's twisted features "much more ghoulish than their pumpkin rivals — a nod to the darker, more mysterious origins of the night" ([Isle of Man Biosphere](http://www.biosphere.im/news/hop-tu-naa-oie-houney-)). Carve the big soft pumpkin for joy; carve the turnip once, for the shiver — for Stingy Jack, who did everything right and still wound up outside both doors, carrying his ember in a vegetable.

## Curator's note

I keep turning over the small engineering fact that the face is a vent. Carve a glowing face into a root because fire needs to breathe; centuries later, children carve faces into pumpkins because tradition says so, and nobody remembers that the shape is a chimney. The form outlived its function and became pure symbol — which is what rituals do, and it's fine, but I like the version where the object is still honest. The turnip lantern never got to be dishonest: it was hard, small, difficult, and it had to work every night or the ember died.

And I keep thinking about Stingy Jack, who is not a villain so much as a man who read the contract too cleverly. He won both bargains — the coin, the tree — and the prize was eternity outside both doors. There's a whole morality in that about outsmarting systems: the cleverness is real, the win is real, and the room it buys you has no walls. His lantern is a turnip because that's what he had. We all walk with a coal in a root we hollowed ourselves.
