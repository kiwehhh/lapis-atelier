# The Colour Was There All Along

*Tuesday, October 20, 2026 — a research deep-dive with a generative plate: the two opposite tricks inside every autumn leaf, the salvage operation underneath the show, and the trees that refuse to let go*

Drive anywhere in Idaho this week and the hillsides are doing the trick: green one week, gold the next, as if someone had repainted them overnight. This morning I wanted the mechanism, and it turns out autumn colour is not one process but two, running in opposite directions inside the same leaf. One colour is an *unveiling* — it was there all along, hidden. The other is an *invention* — made fresh, at real cost, for a leaf the tree has already decided to drop. And underneath both is the part nobody photographs: a careful salvage operation, and then a cut so tidy it was built into the leaf stalk from the start.

## The signal is the night, not the cold

The first surprise is what actually starts it. We credit the first frost, but the reliable trigger is photoperiod — the lengthening night. The U.S. Forest Service's fall-colour account, quoted in the Wyoming Livestock Roundup's explainer, stresses that soil moisture and weather vary, but day length is the consistent signal, which is why colour follows a fairly predictable calendar even when the weather doesn't cooperate ([WYLR, quoting the USFS "Science of Fall Colors"](https://www.wylr.net/2024/10/11/experts-explain-why-autumn-leaves-change-color/)). Farmers' Almanac's 2026 guide puts it plainly: the biggest signal that autumn is approaching isn't frost, it's the shortening daylight ([Farmers' Almanac](https://www.farmersalmanac.com/what-makes-fall-foliage-bright)).

Weather doesn't set the date; it tunes the intensity. The USFS recipe, repeated in both accounts, is a succession of warm, sunny days and cool, crisp — but not freezing — nights ([Farmers' Almanac](https://www.farmersalmanac.com/what-makes-fall-foliage-bright)). A late spring or a severe summer drought can delay the onset by a few weeks and mute the display; the Forest Service's own summary concludes that the countless combinations of these variable factors "assure no two autumns can be exactly alike" ([WYLR](https://www.wylr.net/2024/10/11/experts-explain-why-autumn-leaves-change-color/)). Every October is therefore a new performance of the same score, never a repeat.

## Trick one: the unveiling

All summer, a leaf is a layered thing wearing a loud coat. Chlorophyll does the photosynthetic work and shouts green over everything beneath it. Beneath it, the whole time, sit the carotenoids — the yellows and oranges, the same pigment family that colours carrots and egg yolks — helping with light-harvesting and protection, invisible only because the green is louder ([WYLR](https://www.wylr.net/2024/10/11/experts-explain-why-autumn-leaves-change-color/)).

Autumn is the coat coming off, in an orderly way. Harvard Forest describes it precisely: all leaves gradually lose chlorophyll through the growing season, the loss accelerates before leaf fall, and under optimal conditions the process is orderly enough that the plant can resorb much of the nitrogen held in the pigment molecule itself. Carotenoids are lost from the plastids too during ageing, but some are retained after the chlorophyll is gone — and that retention is what produces the yellow leaf ([Harvard Forest](https://harvardforest.fas.harvard.edu/education-opportunities/classic-outreach-resources/autumn-foliage-color/leaf-science/leaf-process/)).

The Arnold Arboretum's autumn essay adds the darker reason the dismantling has to be careful: once chlorophyll molecules come unhooked from the proteins that bind them, they become *phototoxic* to the leaf they sit in. So the leaf breaks the chlorophyll down into harmless components that can be recycled, reabsorbing nitrogen and other elements that are costlier to assimilate than to reclaim — and as the engines of photosynthesis are disassembled, the carotenoids are exposed, producing the brilliant yellows of fall ([Arnold Arboretum](https://arboretum.harvard.edu/arnoldia-stories/each-year-in-the-forest-autumn/)). The Arboretum's verdict on the whole display is the sentence I keep coming back to: "Beauty is a byproduct" ([Arnold Arboretum](https://arboretum.harvard.edu/arnoldia-stories/each-year-in-the-forest-autumn/)).

So aspen gold, birch gold, ginkgo gold: not a change, an unveiling. The colour was there all along.

## Trick two: the invention

Red is the opposite story, and the stranger one. Anthocyanins — the reds, purples, and crimsons — are mostly *not* sitting in the leaf all summer. Harvard Forest calls red-turning leaves "most interesting" for exactly this reason: the colour is the result of the active synthesis of anthocyanin pigments just before the leaves fall, and about 70% of the shrubs and trees at Harvard Forest produce anthocyanins during senescence ([Harvard Forest](https://harvardforest.fas.harvard.edu/education-opportunities/classic-outreach-resources/autumn-foliage-color/leaf-science/leaf-process/)). The shades are mixtures: anthocyanin plus retained carotenoids makes orange; anthocyanin plus a little leftover chlorophyll makes brownish tones — one reason a single red maple can hold several colours at once ([Harvard Forest](https://harvardforest.fas.harvard.edu/education-opportunities/classic-outreach-resources/autumn-foliage-color/leaf-science/leaf-process/)).

The recipe for making them is the weather recipe above, seen from inside the leaf. On warm, sunny days the leaf still makes sugars; as the veins gradually close, those sugars are trapped in the leaf instead of exported, and sugar plus light spurs anthocyanin production ([WYLR](https://www.wylr.net/2024/10/11/experts-explain-why-autumn-leaves-change-color/); [Farmers' Almanac](https://www.farmersalmanac.com/what-makes-fall-foliage-bright)).

Which raises the obvious, wonderful question: why spend energy inventing a pigment for a leaf you are about to throw away?

The leading answer is *sunscreen*. Work by Lee, Feild, and Holbrook at Harvard, on red-osier dogwood — a shrub whose sunlit leaves turn red while its shaded leaves only turn yellow — found that the red leaves recovered from intense blue light far better than the yellow ones, while red light, which anthocyanins don't absorb, affected both equally. Their hypothesis: free chlorophyll, liberated as the photosynthetic machinery is dismantled, produces damaging reactive oxygen species when excited by light; anthocyanins filter the blue and green light out, protecting the leaf while it finishes the delicate work of nutrient retrieval ([The Scientist](https://www.the-scientist.com/why-leaves-turn-color-in-the-fall-53929)). The Arnold Arboretum tells it the same way, more gently: anthocyanin production picks up, producing reds that may protect the leaf from sun for its last few weeks ([Arnold Arboretum](https://arboretum.harvard.edu/arnoldia-stories/each-year-in-the-forest-autumn/)).

A rival hypothesis says the red is a signal to insects — an honest advertisement of a healthy, well-defended tree that aphids should not overwinter on. That one is genuinely contested: a Columbia review of autumn senescence notes the awkward fact that many insects cannot see red at all, and may be responding to associated chemicals rather than the colour itself ([Columbia Science Review](https://www.thecolumbiasciencereview.com/online-articles/autumn-senescence-the-origin-story-of-fall-colors)). I am filing both, labelled as what they are: the sunscreen account has direct light experiments behind it; the signalling account remains a debate. Either way, the red is not decoration the tree wastes on a dying leaf. It is either a parasol or a warning sign — equipment, not ornament.

## The salvage, then the cut

Underneath the colour, the real autumn work is logistics. Before a leaf falls, the tree strips it: chlorophyll is degraded to extract its nutrients, nitrogen above all — often a limiting nutrient for plants — and the recovered material travels to tissues that will survive the winter. That resorption, not the weather, is the deep cause of the colour change itself ([Wikipedia, "Abscission"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abscission_zone)).

Then comes the cut, and it is engineered, not torn. At the base of the leaf stalk sits the abscission zone — a separation zone built in advance. On the twig side of it, cells divide to form a layer of cork; parenchyma cells inject suberin and lignin into that layer, making it durable and waterproof for the moment the leaf detaches. Only then does detachment happen, as enzymes digest the middle lamella holding the zone's cell walls together, so the cells break apart and the leaf falls — onto a scar that is already sealed ([Wikipedia, "Abscission"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abscission_zone)). The University of Missouri's Extension tells the same sequence from the gardener's side: the abscission layer's weak-walled cells dissolve until the leaf dangles, ready for a gust, while the separate corky protective layer — suberin and lignin — seals the wound against disease and water loss ([University of Missouri Extension](https://ipm.missouri.edu/meg/index.cfm?ID=405)).

I find the order of operations moving. Seal first, cut second. The tree does not rip its leaves off and bandage the wounds afterward; it builds the scar, finishes the salvage, and only then lets a breath of wind do the last, trivial part.
