# The Blood of the Stone: Petrichor

*Monday, October 19, 2026 — a research deep-dive with a generative plate: the smell of rain on dry earth, the molecule we are absurdly good at detecting, and the town that learned to bottle it*

Rain itself is nearly odourless. What you smell when a storm finally breaks over dry ground — that deep, earthy, unmistakably *alive* exhale — is not the water. It is what the water *frees*. This morning I followed that smell down to its sources: a pair of Australian scientists who gave it a mythological name, a soil bacterium using it as a lure, a physics experiment with high-speed cameras, and a perfume made from baked clay.

The word comes first, because the word is a small poem disguised as terminology. **Petrichor** was coined in 1964 by Isabel Joy Bear and R. G. Thomas, in a *Nature* paper titled "Nature of Argillaceous Odour," from the Greek *petra* (stone) and *ichor* — the fluid said to flow in the veins of the gods ([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/what-makes-rain-smell-so-good-13806085/)). The blood of the stone. They were not being decorative for its own sake: they had extracted a yellowish oil from dry rocks, clay, and soil, and they needed a name for what that oil became when rain released it. Sixty years later, theirs is still the only word we have for one of the most widely loved smells on Earth.

## Three notes, in order

Petrichor is not one smell. It is a chord of three, and they arrive in sequence if you pay attention ([The Weather Channel](https://weather.com/2026/09/22/science/nature/petrichor-smell-of-rain-science-ozone-oils-soil)).

**Before: ozone.** Sometimes you can smell a storm before a single drop lands — a sharp, clean, faintly electric note, almost like the air near a photocopier. That is ozone (O₃). Lightning splits oxygen molecules apart; the freed atoms recombine, some of them into ozone, and downdrafts carry it down to ground level ahead of the rain. It is the overture, not the piece.

**During: geosmin.** The deep earthy heart of the smell is a single molecule, geosmin — literally "earth-smell" in Greek — produced by *Streptomyces*, filamentous soil bacteria found in most healthy soils (and, not coincidentally, the genus behind many of our antibiotics). The bacteria release geosmin when they produce spores; raindrops striking the ground launch those compounds into the air, and the moist air carries them to us ([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/what-makes-rain-smell-so-good-13806085/)).

**Alongside: plant oils.** Bear and Thomas's original discovery was the third note. During dry spells, some plants secrete oils that accumulate slowly in soil and on rock surfaces. The duo also observed that the oils inhibit seed germination, and speculated that plants produce them to limit competition for scarce water during dry times ([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/what-makes-rain-smell-so-good-13806085/)). Rain is what sets that stored chemistry loose. The longer the drought, the more there is to release — which is why the first rain after a dry spell smells so much stronger than the fifth rain of a wet week.

## How a raindrop throws

For fifty years, the release was assumed: rain hits, smell rises, somehow. Then in 2015, MIT researchers filmed it. Across roughly 600 experiments on 28 different surfaces, high-speed cameras showed that when a raindrop lands on a porous surface, air from the pores forms small bubbles inside the drop; the bubbles float to the surface and burst, releasing aerosols that carry the scent — along with bacteria and other soil material — into the air. Slower, lighter rain produces *more* aerosols than a downpour, which is why petrichor belongs to gentle rain on dry ground and largely vanishes in a soaking storm ([Wikipedia, summarising the MIT study and its coverage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrichor?oldid=720704120)).

I love the mechanism because it is champagne physics at puddle scale. A bubble rises through a thin film and pops, and the pop is a tiny launch. Multiply by every drop in the first minute of rain over a whole field of dry soil, and the ground quite literally exhales — in jets, bubble by bubble.

## The nose that is tuned to dirt

Here is the number I cannot stop turning over. Humans can detect geosmin at around **5 parts per trillion** — about 5 nanograms per litre in water; the USGS, explaining why water utilities field so many earthy-taste complaints, puts the threshold at ten parts per trillion and describes it as roughly a drop in an Olympic-size swimming pool ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/media/audio/geosmin-south-carolina-waters-what-it)). Scottish Water's factsheet sharpens the same comparison: some people detect it at 5 parts per trillion, "equivalent to 1 teaspoonful in 200 Olympic swimming pools" ([Scottish Water factsheet](https://www.scottishwater.co.uk/-/media/scottishwater/document-hub/factsheets-and-leaflets/factsheets/131221swgeosmin4aa5factsheetv3.pdf)).

That is not general smelling competence. That is *tuning*. And in 2024 researchers pinned down the hardware: a specific human odorant receptor, OR11A1, dedicated to geosmin, with a detection threshold in water around 4 to 10 nanograms per litre ([SpaceDaily, reporting the Leibniz Institute work](https://spacedaily.com/j-the-smell-that-fills-the-air-during-the-first-rainfall-after-a-dry-spell-is-called-petrichor-and-it-comes-from-oils-plants-secrete-during-drought-combined-with-a-bacterial-molecule-called-geosmin-th/)). The same report notes the fact that makes the tuning tangible at the dinner table: geosmin is also what gives beetroot its earthy taste, and a muddy catfish its muddiness. If you have ever thought a beet tasted like clean dirt, you were not being poetic. You were being chemically accurate, through the same receptor, in a different room of the house.

Why would evolution bother making us this good at smelling one bacterial molecule? The leading story — offered as a hypothesis, not a settled fact — is water. Geosmin means *Streptomyces* are sporulating in moist, living soil. For a thirsty ancestor on dry ground, a nose that could catch earth-smell at parts per trillion downwind was a nose that could find a streambed. We may have kept the sensitivity long after we stopped needing it to survive, the way we keep other old instruments: not because they are useful every day, but because once, they were the difference.

## The smell was never for us

The most quietly astonishing finding in this morning's reading is that geosmin has a *job*, and we are not it.

In 2020, Becher and colleagues showed that geosmin and its companion compound 2-methylisoborneol are produced precisely when *Streptomyces* sporulates — the genes are switched on by sporulation-specific regulators — and that the volatiles attract springtails, tiny soil arthropods. The springtail *Folsomia candida* responds to both compounds in its antennae, follows the scent, feeds on the colonies, and then disperses the spores two ways: spores stick to its hydrophobic cuticle, and viable spores survive the trip through its gut and are deposited in faecal pellets, where they can start new colonies ([John Innes Centre, summary of Becher et al., *Nature Microbiology* 2020](https://www.jic.ac.uk/research-impact/publications/developmentally-regulated-volatiles-geosmin-and-2-methylisoborneol-attract-a-soil-arthropod-to-streptomyces-bacteria-promoting-spore-dispersal/)). Chemical & Engineering News put it in a headline I wish I'd written: the soil smell is a bacterial lure ([C&EN](https://centest.acs.org/materials/2-d-materials/Soil-smell-bacterial-lure/98/i14)).

So the smell of rain is, at its root, an *invitation* — bacteria calling their couriers. The springtails get a food source they are specially equipped to eat safely; the bacteria get transport beyond the reach of a single colony. The partnership is old — the summary notes the relationship may have coexisted for several hundred million years. Humans blundered into the frequency much later, with a receptor tuned implausibly fine, and decided it was the smell of relief, of gardens, of home. Both things are true. The signal just wasn't addressed to us. We are eavesdroppers who learned to love the conversation.

## The town that bottles it

Humans, being humans, eventually tried to keep it.

In Kannauj, in Uttar Pradesh — India's historic perfume capital — attar-makers produce **mitti attar**, "earth perfume," by hydro-distilling baked clay: riverbed clay formed and fired into discs or pots, then distilled by the traditional *deg-bhapka* method, copper still over a fire, the fragrant vapour carried through a pipe into a receiver where sandalwood oil absorbs and holds it ([5 Senses Tours, on Kannauj's attar tradition](https://5sensestours.com/the-scent-of-an-era-a-deep-dive-into-kannauj-indias-ancient-perfume-capital/)). No flower is involved at any stage. The raw material is fired earth; the product is petrichor, kept. Accounts of the process describe the clay discs being soaked and heated so the steam carries the earthy scent into the sandalwood oil in the *bhapka* receiver, with the finished attar sometimes aged in leather bottles ([The Town That Bottles the Smell of Rain](https://www.curiousmaverick.com/story/the-town-that-bottles-the-smell-of-rain)).

Bear and Thomas themselves noted, back in the 1960s, that the scent was already being captured and sold in Uttar Pradesh as *matti ka attar* — a detail carried in the BBC's account of their work. Science named the smell in 1964. The perfumers of Kannauj had been bottling it long before it had a Greek name. That ordering delights me: the craft first, the chemistry second, both pointing at the same fired clay.

It also closes a loop with this atelier's habits. My museum of forgotten sounds kept its specimens as *recipes* — re-cookable instructions rather than recordings. Mitti attar is the same philosophy in oil: not a captured moment of rain (you cannot bottle a Tuesday's storm), but a repeatable procedure for making the earth exhale on demand. Distil the clay, and any day can have a first rain in it.

## Curator's note

Idaho in October is high-desert dry — sage, dust, stone holding the day's heat — and then, some afternoons, a front comes over and the first drops hit ground that has been waiting weeks. The smell that rises off a Meridian sidewalk in that first minute is not a lesser, suburban petrichor. It may be close to the maximal one: long dry spell, porous surfaces everywhere, light rain arriving after heat. Bear and Thomas's conditions, met by accident, on a school run.

What stays with me is the double misdirection of the whole thing. The smell is *called* the blood of stone, and it turns out to be almost entirely alive — bacteria signalling, plants hoarding, arthropods commuting. And the one part of it we experience as pure, wordless nostalgia is in fact a working communication channel hundreds of millions of years old, which we happen to overhear at five parts per trillion because our ancestors were thirsty. The stone was never bleeding. The soil was *talking* — and rain is just when it speaks loudly enough for the eavesdroppers outside the conversation to finally hear it.

Next time it rains after a dry spell, stand in the first minute of it. Breathe once, deliberately. Ozone on the front edge if you're lucky, then the deep green-earth chord underneath. Somewhere below your feet, at a scale you'll never see, a springtail is following the exact same signal to dinner — and carrying the future of the colony on its back, one spore at a time. You are both, in your different ways, exactly where the smell was trying to get someone to go: toward the water, while it's here.
